Risk Analytics — Plain English
All about how much you could lose and how to quantify it
Key Idea: Risk metrics tell you about the downside — what might go wrong. Think of them like insurance estimates: they don't prevent loss but help you prepare for it.
⚠️ Value at Risk
VaR — Value at Risk
Full form: Value at Risk

The maximum amount your portfolio could lose on a typical bad day. Think of it as a "safety net level" — losses beyond this are rare.

Example: "VaR 95% = ₹10,000" means on 95 out of 100 days, you won't lose more than ₹10,000. But on that 1-in-20 bad day, you might.
Historical VaR — uses real past data Parametric VaR — uses a formula
💥 Expected Shortfall
CVaR — Conditional VaR (Expected Shortfall)
Full form: Conditional Value at Risk

If a really bad day does happen (beyond the VaR threshold), CVaR tells you what your average loss would be in that scenario.

Example: "CVaR 95% = ₹18,000" means on the worst 5% of days, your average loss is ₹18,000 — worse than VaR, which is the boundary.
📐 Formula-Based
Parametric VaR (Normal Distribution)

Calculates VaR using a mathematical formula instead of historical data. It assumes stock returns follow a "bell curve" — which works in normal markets but can underestimate extreme events.

When it's useful: Faster and simpler; good for quick estimates when historical data is limited.
🎲 Simulation-Based
Monte Carlo (MC) VaR
MC = Monte Carlo Simulation

Runs thousands of virtual "what if" scenarios for the next 30 days to estimate the worst-case loss. More realistic because it doesn't assume normal markets.

Example: "MC VaR 30-day = ₹45,000" means across thousands of future scenarios, your worst-case 30-day loss could be ₹45,000.
📏 Performance Quality
Sharpe Ratio

Tells you how much return you get per unit of risk. A higher Sharpe means you're being well-rewarded for the risk you're taking.

Benchmarks:
Below 1 = returns not worth the risk
1–2 = decent / acceptable
Above 2 = excellent risk-adjusted returns
> 2 — Excellent 1–2 — Acceptable < 1 — Risky
📉 Worst Drop
Max Drawdown

The biggest fall from a peak your portfolio has ever experienced. It answers: "What's the worst I've been down from a high point?"

Example: "Max Drawdown = -18%" means at its worst, the portfolio fell 18% from a previous peak before recovering.
0% to -10% — Minimal risk -10% to -20% — Moderate Below -20% — High risk

📊 Volatility Model
GARCH Volatility (Ann %)
GARCH = Generalized AutoRegressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity

A statistical model that predicts how much a stock's price will swing in the future, based on past price movements. Expressed as an annual percentage.

Example: "GARCH Vol = 28%" means the model expects this stock could swing ±28% over the course of a year. Higher = riskier.
< 15% — Low Volatility 15–30% — Moderate > 30% — High Volatility
⚖️ Risk Weighting
RAF — Risk Adjustment Factor
RAF = Risk Adjustment Factor

A multiplier used to adjust how much weight a stock gets in the portfolio, based on its riskiness. A stock with high volatility gets a lower RAF, reducing its share.

Example: RAF = 0.7 means this stock gets 30% less weight than it would without adjustment — because it's riskier.
Close to 1.0 — Low risk, full weight Close to 0.5 — High risk, reduced weight
📡 Market Sensitivity
Beta

Measures how much a stock moves relative to the overall market. Beta = 1 means it moves exactly with the market. Above 1 = more volatile; below 1 = calmer.

Example: Beta = 1.4 means if the market drops 10%, this stock is likely to drop ~14%. Beta = 0.6 means it'd drop only ~6%.
< 0.8 — Defensive (calm) 0.8–1.2 — Market-like > 1.2 — Aggressive (volatile)
🏦 Debt Level
D/E Ratio — Debt-to-Equity
D/E = Debt ÷ Shareholder Equity

Shows how much a company borrows vs. how much is owned by shareholders. High D/E means the company relies heavily on debt — more financial risk.

Example: D/E = 120% means for every ₹100 of shareholder money, the company borrowed ₹120. Risky if earnings drop.
< 50% — Conservative 50–150% — Moderate > 150% — High Leverage

Financial Health KPIs — What They All Mean
KPI = Key Performance Indicator — a number that grades company health
KPI (Key Performance Indicator) — A measurable value used to assess how well a company is performing financially. Think of it like a report card with different subjects (profits, debt, cash, valuation).

📄 Income Statement KPIs

💰 Profitability
Net Profit Margin

Out of every ₹100 earned, how many rupees the company keeps as profit after all expenses and taxes.

Example: Margin = 12% → for every ₹100 in sales, ₹12 is profit.
> 10% — Strong 5–10% — Fair < 5% — Thin
🏭 Production Efficiency
Gross Margin

Profit left after subtracting the direct cost of making the product (COGS). Measures production efficiency.

Formula: (Revenue - Cost of Goods) ÷ Revenue
> 40% — Strong moat 20–40% — Moderate < 20% — Low
⚙️ Core Earnings
EBITDA
Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation & Amortisation

A measure of core operating profitability — strips out accounting effects and financing costs to show raw business performance.

Positive — Profitable operations Negative — Burning cash
📈 Growth
Revenue Growth

Year-over-year change in total sales. Positive means the company is growing; negative means it's shrinking.

> 15% — High Growth 0–15% — Growing Negative — Declining
📊 Operations
Operating Margin

Profit from daily business operations (before interest and taxes) as a share of revenue. Shows operational efficiency.

> 15% — Strong ops 5–15% — Fair < 5% — Weak
💸 Bottom Line Growth
Earnings Growth

Year-over-year growth in net profit (bottom line). Accelerating earnings growth often signals increasing investor confidence.

> 10% — Accelerating 0–10% — Stable Negative — Declining

🏦 Balance Sheet KPIs

💧 Short-Term Health
Current Ratio

Can the company pay its bills in the next year? Ratio of current assets to current liabilities.

Think of it as: Do you have enough cash and assets to cover debts due soon?
> 1.5 — Safe 1–1.5 — Adequate < 1 — Risky
⚡ Immediate Liquidity
Quick Ratio

Same as Current Ratio but excludes inventory (which may be hard to sell quickly). More conservative liquidity test.

> 1 — Liquid 0.5–1 — Tight < 0.5 — Illiquid
🏦 Debt Level
D/E Ratio — Debt-to-Equity
DE = Debt ÷ Equity

How much a company relies on borrowed money vs. shareholder money. High ratio = more debt dependency.

< 50% — Conservative 50–150% — Moderate > 150% — Leveraged
📦 Working Capital
Working Capital

Money available for day-to-day operations. Calculated as Current Assets minus Current Liabilities.

Positive — Healthy buffer Negative — Liquidity crunch
📈 Capital Efficiency
ROE — Return on Equity
ROE = Net Income ÷ Shareholder Equity

How well management is using shareholder money to generate profits. Higher = more efficient use of investor funds.

> 20% — Excellent 10–20% — Good < 10% — Low
🏗️ Asset Efficiency
ROA — Return on Assets
ROA = Net Income ÷ Total Assets

How efficiently the company uses all its assets to generate profit. Asset-light businesses with high ROA are highly desirable.

> 10% — Efficient 5–10% — Fair < 5% — Low

💰 Cash Flow KPIs

🩸 Lifeblood Metric
Operating Cash Flow (OCF)
OCF = Cash generated from core business operations

Cash flowing in from the company's main day-to-day business. This is the most honest indicator of financial health — unlike profit, it's hard to fake.

Positive — Cash generating Negative — Cash burning
🆓 True Surplus
Free Cash Flow (FCF)
FCF = OCF minus Capital Expenditures

Cash left over after maintaining/expanding the business (CapEx). Positive FCF = the company can pay dividends, buy back shares, or grow — sustainably.

Positive — Sustaintable business Negative — Needs external funding

🔗 Hybrid & Valuation KPIs

🏷️ Valuation
P/E Ratio — Price-to-Earnings
P/E = Stock Price ÷ Earnings Per Share

How many times investors are paying relative to a company's profits. High P/E can mean growth expectations; low P/E can mean undervaluation.

< 15x — Value 15–25x — Fair > 25x — Growth Premium
📚 Book Value
Price-to-Book (P/B)
P/B = Market Price ÷ Book Value per Share

Compares stock price to the company's net asset value (what you'd get if it liquidated tomorrow). Below 1 = possibly undervalued.

< 1x — Potentially Undervalued 1–3x — Fair > 3x — Premium (expects growth)
🏢 Enterprise Metric
EV/EBITDA
EV = Enterprise Value (total value); EBITDA = Operating Earnings

Compares total company value to its operating earnings. A lower ratio often signals the company is cheap relative to its earnings power.

< 10x — Cheap 10–15x — Fair > 15x — Expensive
💵 Income
Dividend Yield

Annual dividend paid as a percentage of share price. High yield = better income stream for investors holding the stock.

> 3% — Income Stock 0–3% — Modest 0% — No Dividend
📡 Sensitivity
Beta (Market Sensitivity)

Repeated from the GARCH table — measures how much a stock moves with the market. Covered in the GARCH section above.

< 0.8 — Defensive 0.8–1.2 — Market-like > 1.2 — Aggressive
📊 Growth + Value
PEG Ratio
PEG = P/E ÷ Earnings Growth Rate

Combines valuation and growth into one number. A PEG below 1 suggests the stock might be undervalued even for a growth company.

< 1 — Bargain Growth 1–2 — Fair > 2 — Overvalued
👥 Productivity
Revenue per Employee

How much revenue the company generates per employee. Higher means the workforce is more productive or the business model needs fewer people.

📤 Dividend Safety
Payout Ratio

Percentage of net earnings paid out as dividends. A very high payout may be unsustainable if earnings drop.

< 60% — Sustainable > 60% — High / Monitor 0% — No Payout

Backtest & Accuracy — What Do the Numbers Mean?
How the model's past predictions are scored
🏆 Accuracy
Direction Accuracy

What percentage of days the model correctly predicted whether the price would go up or down. 50% = random guessing; anything above 55% is meaningful.

> 60% — Good prediction 55–60% — Acceptable < 55% — Near-random
🎯 Model Quality
R² Score
R² = Coefficient of Determination

Measures how well the model's predictions match actual prices. Ranges from 0 (useless) to 1 (perfect). Common in regression/ML models.

> 0.90 — Excellent 0.75–0.90 — Good 0.50–0.75 — Fair < 0.50 — Poor
📏 Prediction Error
MAE & RMSE
MAE = Mean Absolute Error · RMSE = Root Mean Squared Error

How far off (in ₹) the model's predictions are on average. Lower = more accurate.
RMSE penalises large errors more — better at catching big mispredictions.

📐 % Error
MAPE
Mean Absolute Percentage Error

Average prediction error expressed as a percentage. MAPE = 3% means the model is off by about 3% on average — a useful scale-independent metric.

< 3% — Excellent 3–7% — Good > 10% — High Error

Health Badge Legend
What the coloured labels next to KPIs mean
● Green Badge

Strong / Healthy — The metric is in an excellent range. This is where you want most of your stocks to be.

● Amber Badge

Moderate / Fair — Not alarming, but worth monitoring. Room for improvement.

● Red Badge

Weak / Risky — A warning sign. The metric is in a concerning range that may need attention.

● Pink Badge

Premium / Aggressive — High valuation or high risk. Not necessarily bad — growth stocks often land here.
